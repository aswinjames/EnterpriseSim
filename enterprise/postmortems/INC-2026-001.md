## Summary

At 18:00 UTC on 2026-01-14, the Redis primary backing the Cart Service (APP-004) underwent
an unplanned failover. Cart write operations (add-to-cart, quantity update, cart merge)
returned elevated error rates for approximately 9 minutes while a replica was promoted to
primary and clients re-established connections. Read traffic served from replicas was
largely unaffected. No orders were lost; the checkout path (APP-003) degraded gracefully by
retrying against the newly promoted primary. The incident was declared resolved at 18:17
UTC once cart write error rate returned to baseline and connection pools stabilized.

## Impact

- **Users:** Shoppers in the NA region attempting to modify their carts saw intermittent
  "could not update cart" errors for a ~9 minute window. Estimated ~4% of active NA cart
  sessions during the window experienced at least one failed write.
- **Regions:** NA only.
- **Revenue-proxy:** Estimated revenue-proxy exposure of ~$18K, driven by abandoned cart
  modifications during the window. Because carts persist and retries succeeded after
  promotion, most sessions recovered; realized loss is believed to be a small fraction of
  exposure.
- **Data integrity:** No cart data loss. Redis AOF persistence and the replica promotion
  preserved committed cart state.

## Root cause

**Category: capacity.** The Redis primary was operating close to its memory ceiling due to
organic growth in active cart volume ahead of a promotional period. Rising memory pressure
degraded the node until it failed its health check, triggering an automatic Sentinel
failover. The failover itself worked as designed, but the client-side connection pool
reconnect behavior was slower than the promotion, producing a ~9 minute window in which a
minority of cart writes failed before pools converged on the new primary. The trigger was
**organic** (load-driven), not a code or config change.

Contributing factors:

- Redis instance sizing had not been revisited since the prior quarter despite steady cart
  volume growth; headroom alerts were set at a threshold that fired too late to pre-empt.
- The Cart Service client used a conservative connection-pool refresh interval, lengthening
  the reconnect tail after promotion.

## Detection

Automated. The SLO alert on APP-004 cart-write error rate crossed threshold within seconds
of the primary going unresponsive, and the incident was opened automatically at 18:00 UTC.
Detection was healthy; no gap between failure and alert.

## Resolution

The incident self-resolved through the designed Sentinel failover: a replica was promoted
to primary and Cart Service clients reconnected. SRE and TEAM-004 monitored error-rate
recovery and confirmed no cart data loss. No hotfix PR was required — the mitigation was
operational (verify promotion, confirm pool convergence). Follow-up capacity work was
scheduled as action items rather than an emergency change.

## What went well

- Automatic failover and alerting both functioned as designed; detection was immediate.
- Read path stayed healthy throughout; the blast radius was confined to cart writes.
- No cart data was lost; persistence and replication held.
- Checkout (APP-003) degraded gracefully and retried, limiting downstream impact.

## What went wrong

- Redis capacity headroom was insufficient for organic growth; the node was allowed to
  drift toward its memory ceiling before any proactive scaling.
- Headroom alerting thresholds fired too late to prevent the failover.
- The client connection-pool reconnect tail extended the user-visible error window beyond
  the promotion time itself.

## Lessons learned

- Capacity headroom for stateful data stores must track organic growth proactively; alerts
  set only at near-failure thresholds convert routine growth into incidents.
- A correctly functioning failover can still produce user-visible errors if client reconnect
  behavior is not tuned to match promotion speed — resilience is a property of the whole
  path, not just the data store.
- Confining blast radius (reads unaffected, checkout retrying) is what kept a Sev2 from
  becoming a Sev1; graceful degradation on dependent services is worth the investment.

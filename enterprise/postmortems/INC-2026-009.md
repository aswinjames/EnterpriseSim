## Summary

At 20:00 UTC on 2026-03-04, MCG's primary external Payment Service Provider (PSP) began
degrading, with sharply elevated authorization response times. The Payments Service
(APP-012) authorization p99 spiked as calls to the primary PSP slowed and a portion timed
out. Checkout completions slowed and a minority of authorizations failed and had to be
retried. Failover routing to the secondary PSP engaged (initially requiring on-call
confirmation), after which authorization latency recovered. No cardholder data was exposed
and no double-charges occurred; PCI boundaries held throughout.

## Impact

- **Users:** Shoppers in NA and LATAM experienced slow or briefly failed payment
  authorizations at checkout for up to ~50 minutes, with the worst latency in the first
  half of the window before failover engaged.
- **Regions:** NA and LATAM.
- **Revenue-proxy:** Estimated ~$85K exposure from delayed/abandoned checkouts during the
  degradation; most sessions recovered on retry or after failover, so realized loss is a
  fraction of exposure.
- **Data integrity / compliance:** No cardholder data exposure; PCI boundary (APP-012)
  intact. No confirmed double-charges; idempotency keys on authorization calls prevented
  duplicate captures on retry.

## Root cause

**Category: third_party.** The root cause was external: MCG's primary PSP suffered a
provider-side degradation that elevated authorization latency and caused timeouts. APP-012
behaved correctly given a slow dependency, but the **failover to the secondary PSP was not
fully automatic** — it required on-call confirmation, which added minutes to the impact
window during which authorizations remained slow. The contributing internal factor was thus
the manual step in an otherwise sound multi-PSP resilience design.

## Detection

Automated. The authorization p99 latency SLO alert fired at the onset. Detection was healthy.
The improvement opportunity is in mean-time-to-mitigate (automating failover), not
mean-time-to-detect.

## Resolution

Traffic was failed over to the secondary PSP for the affected regions, after which
authorization latency recovered. The primary PSP was monitored for provider-side recovery
before any fail-back. No code change shipped during the incident; the mitigation was a
routing action. Idempotency keys ensured retries did not double-charge.

## What went well

- Multi-PSP architecture existed and worked — a secondary provider was available to absorb
  traffic.
- Idempotency keys on authorization calls prevented double-charges during retries.
- PCI boundaries and cardholder-data isolation held throughout.
- Detection was immediate via the latency SLO.

## What went wrong

- PSP failover required manual confirmation, extending the impact window; the resilience
  design was sound but not automatic.
- Circuit-breaker/timeout tuning on the primary-PSP client allowed too many slow calls
  before shedding, worsening early-window latency.

## Lessons learned

- Multi-provider resilience only pays off at the speed of its slowest human step; failover
  for Tier 0 dependencies should be automated with guardrails, not gated on manual approval.
- Circuit breakers must be tuned to shed a degrading dependency quickly rather than absorb
  its latency.
- Idempotency on payment operations is essential and proved its value here — retries under
  degradation did not create duplicate charges.
